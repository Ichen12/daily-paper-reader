Abstract —DCASE 2026 Task 5 introduces Audio-Dependent Question Answering (ADQA), which tests whether large audio-language models answer from the audio rather than from textual priors. An Audio-Dependency Filtering (ADF) pipeline combines silent-audio probing, per-option perplexity, a large language model (LLM) commonsense check, and human review to remove items solvable from text alone. The 3000 items that pass form the ADQA-Bench evaluation set, spanning music, speech, and environmental audio. The inaugural edition draws 14 teams and 36 submissions across two tracks defined by total parameter count (up to 100B and under 10B). A Chung-Ang University ensemble of MOSS-Audio-8B-Thinking and Qwen3-Omni-30B reaches the top overall accuracy at 58.33 %, and a MOSS-only configuration from the same team leads the sub-10B track at 57.30 %. Across the 30 submissions with a comparable development score, evaluation accuracy falls by 11.91 percentage points (pp) on average (median 10.91 pp) on the hidden evaluation split, which is designed to be harder than the development split. The most common building blocks are: the MOSS-Audio-8B-Thinking backbone (13 of 36 submissions), Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) fine-tuning on AudioMCQ-StrongAC, and preference or reinforcement-learning objectives—Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) in five teams, Group reward-Decoupled Normalization Policy Optimization (GDPO) in two. At test time, prompt engineering is near-universal, and majority or choice-permutation voting is common. Every system misses the same set of 233 evaluation items. 

Index Terms —Audio question answering, large audio-language models, multimodal evaluation, DCASE challenge 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Large Audio-Language Models (LALMs) can inflate their accuracy on multiple-choice audio benchmarks by partly exploiting textual shortcuts rather than the audio itself. Replace the audio with silence and state-of-the-art LALMs still answer many questions correctly, using linguistic priors and dataset artifacts [1]–[4]. Progress requires benchmarks that remove such items, and models that answer from the audio signal itself. DCASE 2026 Task 5, Audio-Dependent Question Answering (ADQA) [1], addresses this gap. An Audio-Dependency Filtering (ADF) pipeline drops questions that a text-only model can solve, leaving a 3000-item evaluation set (ADQA-Bench). Submissions are open-source LALMs that answer four-way multiple-choice questions grounded in the audio. Two tracks run in parallel, split by total parameter count: an Overall track (≤100 B) and a Lightweight track ( < 10 B). The top Overall system, Lim_CAU from Chung-Ang University [5], reaches 58.33 %; the same team wins the Lightweight track at 57.30 %. The strongest organizer baseline, Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B-Instruct (hereafter Qwen3-Omni-30B) [6], scores 62.48 % on development. Baselines are run only on development. Team submissions average

2. TASK SETUP AND DATASET 2.1. Task Definition 

DCASE 2026 Task 5 (ADQA) is a four-way multiple-choice question (MCQ) task over audio clips. Each item pairs one audio recording, one question, and four options with a single correct choice. Every question must be audio-dependent : the correct answer cannot be recovered from the text alone. 

2.2. Audio-Dependency Filtering (ADF) 

The ADF cascade [1] is a four-stage pipeline (Fig. 1) that keeps only items whose correct answer depends on the audio signal. 

Hard-ADF (silent-audio probe). We replace the audio with silence and ask a pool of LALMs to answer. An item is discarded if more than one blind model still picks the correct option, since the answer is then largely recoverable without listening. 

Soft-ADF (perplexity filter). We score each option with language-model perplexity under silent audio. If the correct option has the lowest perplexity, the item is dropped: a text-only preference for the right answer exposes a linguistic shortcut. 

LLM commonsense filter. A text-only large language model (LLM) sees the question and options without the audio. 2.3. Datasets Training set. The official training corpus, AudioMCQ-StrongAC-GeminiCoT (hereafter AudioMCQ-StrongAC), is derived from the 

∼571k-item AudioMCQ corpus [1]. We keep a subset of the Strong Audio-Contribution (StrongAC) split and add chain-of-thought (CoT) rationales distilled from Gemini 3.1 Pro. 

Development set. 1607 MCQ items drawn from existing bench-marks (MMAU [2], MMAR [3], MMSU [4]) together with newly annotated questions, all passing the four-stage ADF. 

Evaluation set. The hidden ADQA-Bench holds 3000 MCQ items filtered under the same protocol. Team-submission scores in this paper refer to this evaluation set, whereas organizer baseline numbers refer to the development set alone (Section 5.2). 

Protocol. Two tracks are defined by total parameter count: Overall (≤100 B total, no component > 30 B) and Lightweight ( < 10 B). Submissions must use open-source LALMs. Manual annotation of evaluation items is forbidden: teams cannot label evaluation audio by hand, re-transcribe it for training, or feed per-item human answers back into their systems. Each team can submit up to four systems. 

2.4. Baseline Systems 

The organizers release five LALM baselines with tuned prompts and score them by top-1 accuracy on the development set. Table 1 reports these scores next to a random-guess level of 25.46 %. This exceeds 25 % likely because option-length and distractor artifacts make a few choices slightly more likely than a uniform guess. Qwen3-Omni-30B scores highest among the baselines. 

3. SUBMISSION OVERVIEW 

The first edition of DCASE 2026 Task 5 draws 14 teams and 36 ranked submissions, spanning 31.87 % to 58.33 % accuracy. Because each system is scored on 3000 items, gaps of about a percentage point should be read as ties. Every system is built on one of six public LALMs, so teams differ mainly in their backbone choice and how they curate data around it. The overall champion is Lim_CAU_4 from Chung-Ang Univer-sity [5], which reaches 58.33 % by ensembling MOSS-Audio-8B-Thinking [11] with Qwen3-Omni-30B [6] (96.0 B in the challenge metadata; the team reports a 92 B effective total over four MOSS-8B and two Qwen3-Omni-30B inference views). Its pipeline chains audio-vs-silent difficulty binning, Group reward-Decoupled Normalization Policy Optimization (GDPO) [12]-based Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) [13], an inference-time acoustic tagger, and choice-permutation ensembling (see Section 4.4). Nam_IND [14] applies 4-bit quantized LoRA (QLoRA) [15] supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with a response normalizer on Qwen3-Omni-30B (60 B, 57.17 %). Table 3 groups submissions by primary backbone. Two models dominate. MOSS-Audio-8B-Thinking [11] (13 submissions, 5 teams) gives the best single-submission accuracy (58.33 %, from a system that also fuses Qwen3-Omni-30B views) and a backbone mean of 55.49 %. 4.4. Winning System: Lim_CAU 

The winning recipe [5] scores each training candidate under matched (audio, silent) prompts and bins them into six difficulty classes. From 167,541 AudioMCQ-StrongAC [1] candidates, 12,800 rows are drawn with group-level quotas. A choice-layout calibration oversamples option D targets (A/B/C/D = 1.0/1.0/1.0/1.3) to counter reported positional bias in MOSS outputs. GDPO [12]-based LoRA [13] then tunes the model. At inference, an acoustic tagger injects librosa [34]-derived evidence from the taggers above. The final submission 

Lim_CAU_4 fuses MOSS and Qwen3-Omni-30B views via weighted log-probs ( λ ≈0.46 ) and reaches 58.33 %, the highest accuracy of any single submission. No ablation isolates the acoustic tagger from the two-backbone ensemble, so its standalone gain is unknown. 

5. PERFORMANCE ANALYSIS 5.1. Question-level Difficulty Distribution 

Figure 3 bins the 3000 evaluation questions by the fraction of submissions that answer correctly. The distribution is bimodal: 567 questions (18.9 %) are solved by fewer than 10 % of systems and 638 (21.3 %) by more than 90 %. 233 items are missed by every ranked system; 110 are solved by all. For 47.6 % of items, fewer than half of the 36 submissions answer correctly, so shared failures reach well past the 233-item universal-failure set. This points to weaknesses shared by the LALM backbones used here; Section 6 revisits their likely causes in detail.

5.3. Reasoning-Time Interventions 

CoT-target SFT raises development accuracy for some systems— 

Hu_IOA ’s Qwen2.5-Omni from 55.88 % to 57.93 % [16] and 

Zhang_WHU ’s MOSS-Audio from 52.21 % to 56.94 % [19]—but three other reasoning-time add-ons do not. A separate training-time case shows the reverse risk: Hu_IOA reports a −8.71 pp development-split drop from SFT on MOSS-Audio-8B-Thinking, with development accuracy falling from 67.70 % to 58.99 %; they attribute the drop to catastrophic forgetting of the model’s native audio reasoning rather than to CoT supervision itself [16]. Nam_IND loses −1.93 pp with a CoT-then-letter prompt [14]. Tathe_UIUC ’s Prometheus [35] CoT judge is statistically tied with its no-judge run (57.31 % vs 58.43 %, McNemar p=0.23 ) [20]. Yin_XJTLU drops −8.96 pp by replacing majority voting with a rule that picks the lowest-entropy branch across its training-free prompt programs [17]. This is a decoding-time change only, so it isolates the selection rule from CoT training. 

6. DISCUSSION AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS 

ADQA-Bench makes audio dependence measurable: submissions span 31.87 %–58.33 % on the evaluation set, and 233 items (7.8 %) are missed by every system (Section 5.1). An oracle picking the best answer across the 36 submissions solves 2767 of 3000 items (92.2 %), yet no single system comes close. Fine-tuning on AudioMCQ-StrongAC derivatives [1], [16], [19], [20] is the most common training approach; Section 5.3 shows reasoning-time interventions give mixed results—CoT-target SFT helps on some backbones, whereas CoT prompting, CoT-based judging, and entropy-based branch selection do not. Better acoustic grounding (cf. Section 4) is a next direction, though no submission isolates its contribution [5], [26]. The dev-to-eval variance (Section 5.2) motivates a held-out tuning split, separate from the development set. Because the development set re-filters MMAU [2], MMAR [3] and MMSU [4] items (Section 2), such a split separates benchmark overlap from overfitting. The 233 universally failed items (Section 5.1) expose capability gaps common to these LALM backbones, pointing to better audio encoders and audio-grounded pre-training. The narrow 1.03 pp gap between the 96 B champion and the 8 B lightweight winner shows data curation and inference-time orchestration rival raw scale. Extending the benchmark to open-ended generation would blunt elimination shortcuts but needs an audio-blind judge.
