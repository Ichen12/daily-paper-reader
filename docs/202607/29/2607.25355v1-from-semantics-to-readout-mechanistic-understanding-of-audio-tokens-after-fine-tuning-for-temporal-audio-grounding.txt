Abstract 

Large audio-language models (LALMs) convey acoustic evi-dence to language decoders through native audio tokens, yet the internal roles of these tokens remain poorly understood. Using temporal audio grounding as a diagnostic setting, we examine how language-model fine-tuning affects the layerwise semantics, decoder accessibility, and temporal output align-ment of native audio-token states through four complementary analyses: query-conditioned token semantics, calibrated to-ken readout, temporal-window probes, and residual-delta era-sure during generation. Alongside substantial improvements in temporal localization, semantic analysis of Qwen2.5-Omni shows that latent evidence for queried events is already present before fine-tuning and that the audio tokens most strongly aligned with the queried event appear at similar temporal po-sitions before and after fine-tuning. After fine-tuning, event-related information in audio tokens becomes more accessible to the decoder, especially in early and middle layers, and a cross-checkpoint control shows that this improvement arises primarily from decoder adaptation. Temporal probes show that the base checkpoint already contains recoverable infor-mation about annotated windows and that fine-tuning mainly improves alignment with each checkpoint’s own predicted temporal support. Residual-delta erasure further shows that re-moving audio-token updates within predicted windows harms timestamp generation more than removing the same number of randomly selected updates. The same broad improvements in decoder readability and prediction alignment also appear in Qwen2-Audio. Together, these results support a semantics-to-readout account in which grounding fine-tuning helps the decoder read existing event evidence and connect it more re-liably to temporal outputs. 

1 Introduction 

Large audio-language models (LALMs) connect acoustic signals with the reasoning and generation capabilities of large language models (Tang et al. 2024; Chu et al. 2024; Kong et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2025). They convert continuous wave-forms into audio tokens, the interface through which acoustic evidence enters language reasoning. Yet these native audio tokens remain poorly understood: they support downstream behavior, but what they encode, when this information be-comes readable, and how it reaches final text remain unclear.

Temporal grounding gives a controlled way to probe this question. General audio understanding asks what event oc-curs; temporal grounding also asks when. This makes task fine-tuning an informative intervention because the model must connect event semantics to explicit temporal outputs. Since general LALMs are not primarily trained for precise timestamp grounding (Tang et al. 2024; Chu et al. 2024), recent evaluations report persistent failures in localization, duration estimation, temporal reasoning, and timestamp pre-diction (Ahia et al. 2025; Kulkarni et al. 2026; Yao et al. 2025). Recent work improves temporal grounding by making time explicit through time-aware audio representations, au-dio prompts, timestamp-interleaved training sequences, or grounding objectives (Wang et al. 2026; Shi et al. 2026; Sun et al. 2026; An et al. 2026). These methods show that stronger temporal supervision and interfaces help. However, they mainly optimize timestamp interfaces and outputs, often using fine-tuned LALMs as baselines before adding explicit timestamp structure. This leaves a key internal question un-derexplored: how does task fine-tuning change the semantics and decoder readout of native audio-token representations? As illustrated in Figure 1, we use fine-tuning for temporal grounding to compare native audio-token states before and after task adaptation. Rather than treating fine-tuning as a black box that simply adds temporal capabilities, we orga-nize the analysis around four linked research questions: RQ1 :What query-relevant event evidence is already present in native audio-token states before grounding fine-tuning, and how stable are its temporal locations across checkpoints? 

RQ2 : How does fine-tuning affect the decoder’s ability to read this evidence, and do the resulting gains arise primar-ily from intermediate-state changes or downstream decoder adaptation? RQ3 : How do audio-token states correspond to annotated event windows and to each checkpoint’s own pre-dicted temporal support? RQ4 : Do residual updates within predicted windows affect timestamp generation? Together, these questions trace the progression from event semantics to decoder readout, temporal output alignment, and generation-time relevance. The main contributions are summarized as follows:

2 Related Work 

Temporal Audio Grounding Temporal audio grounding localizes the time intervals of queried sound events. It is closely related to sound event detection, which localizes pre-defined sound classes from strong or weak temporal labels (Bhosale et al. 2024; Cornell et al. 2024). Text query ground-ing generalizes this setting to natural-language event phrases, as in AudioGrounding’s phrase-level links among clips, cap-tions, event phrases, and timestamp intervals (Xu et al. 2021). Because it conditions on phrases, this formulation naturally fits LALMs, which jointly condition on audio and text and generate textual responses. For LALMs, grounding methods typically improve times-tamp prediction by making time explicit in the interface or objective. TimeAudio and SpotSound use temporal markers, time encodings, timestamp sequences, or specialized tempo-ral supervision (Wang et al. 2026; Sun et al. 2026); TimePro-RL injects timestamp embeddings and optimizes temporal alignment with rewards (Shi et al. 2026); and tool use at the frame level exposes finer temporal operations for audio-language models (An et al. 2026). These works mainly study how temporal coordinates should be represented, supervised, or generated. In contrast, we use temporal grounding as a di-agnostic setting for studying how task fine-tuning changes native audio-token semantics and decoder readout. 

Audio Tokens as Interfaces LALMs follow a common multimodal design in which audio inputs are encoded, pro-jected into the hidden space of the language model, and placed in the decoder context as token representations. Acoustic encoders and projection modules produce native audio-token states that condition text generation, enabling audio question answering, captioning, dialogue, and gen-eral audio understanding (Tang et al. 2024; Chu et al. 2024; Kong et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2025). These states form the internal interface through which acoustic evidence reaches the language decoder. Benchmarks such as AudioBench and AIR-Bench evaluate external capabilities across speech, en-vironmental sound, music, and open-ended audio interaction (Wang et al. 2025; Yang et al. 2024). They do not, however, explain how fine-tuning for a task changes what event ev-idence native audio-token states organize, or whether that evidence becomes more accessible to the language decoder. Token Evidence and Readout Methods for interpreting hidden states provide distinct ways to inspect internal ev-idence and its accessibility. Linear probing tests whether information can be recovered, although recoverability alone does not show that the evidence is used during generation (Hewitt and Liang 2019). LatentLens compares hidden states with contextualized semantic references (Krojer et al. 2026), while Patchscopes use diagnostic prompts to verbalize hid-den states (Ghandeharioun et al. 2024). Causal tracing and activation-patching interventions test whether hidden-state components affect model outputs (Meng et al. 2022). These methods separate recoverability, semantic alignment in rep-resentation space, decoder readout, and functional relevance. Interpretability work in audio has begun to examine inter-nal representations beyond final task accuracy. AudioLens traces auditory attribute evidence in LALMs through projec-tions into vocabulary space (Yang et al. 2025); Beyond Tran-scription uses probing and interventions to study automatic speech recognition representations (Glazer et al. 2026); Ma et al. analyze how LoRA adaptation reshapes Whisper encoder representations for speech emotion recognition through prob-ing, logit-lens, and representation-similarity analyses (Ma et al. 2026); causal tracing examines audio-text fusion across layers and token positions (Chen, Huang, and Lee 2026); and audio-visual stress tests show that multimodal LLMs may fail to surface auditory evidence in generated answers even when sound information is present (Selvakumar et al. 2026). These studies reveal semantic evidence in audio representations and possible gaps between audio information and generated be-havior, but they do not directly examine how task fine-tuning changes native audio-token semantics, decoder readout, and alignment with explicit temporal outputs. We address this gap by treating temporal-grounding fine-tuning as a con-trolled intervention and comparing representation, readout, and output correspondence across checkpoints. 

3 Method 

Our diagnostics compare native audio-token states before and after grounding fine-tuning using query-conditioned seman-tics, calibrated readout, output-facing temporal probes, and an intervention during generation. These diagnostics charac-terize representational alignment, decoder accessibility, out-put consistency, and functional relevance for timestamp gen-eration. 

Audio-Token Interface 

Given an audio clip x and a natural-language audio ques-tion, we denote the full question by q and the target event phrase extracted from it by e(q). The extracted event phrase is used for matching and scoring at the event level. Our anal-ysis focuses on the internal audio-token representations that connect acoustic evidence to textual responses about events, rather than on the final generated answer alone. The processor constructs a mixed audio-text input se-quence containing text tokens from the question and, for the waveform, audio placeholder tokens specific to the model. The audio tower encodes the waveform into audio features, which are projected into representations associated with the corresponding audio-token positions. Let A denote the na-tive audio-token positions in this mixed sequence, identified by the audio-token id used by the model. For the i-th audio token and layer ℓ, we denote its hidden state as haℓ (i), where 

i indexes audio-token positions and ℓ indexes LALM layers. These layerwise audio-token states are the focus of our analysis.

Calibrated Token Readout 

The audio-token semantics diagnostic measures alignment in representation space, but it does not test whether the lan-guage decoder can express the information carried by an audio-token state. We therefore use a calibrated hidden-state insertion diagnostic for audio-token readout: a source audio-token state haℓ (i) is inserted into a sound event prompt to elicit a natural language readout. Residual-delta erasure. To test functional relevance in the original generation context, we apply residual-delta erasure to the fine-tuned Qwen2.5-Omni checkpoint. 4 Experiments 

AudioGrounding-QA 

We construct AudioGrounding-QA by converting phrase-level temporal annotations from the public AudioGround-ing corpus, released with TextToAudioGrounding (Xu et al. 2021), into an audio question-answering format. Audio-Grounding is built on AudioCaps/AudioSet clips (Kim et al. 2019; Gemmeke et al. 2017); each phrase item with timestamped segments becomes one event-centered tempo-ral question and an answer verbalized from the annotated intervals. After QA construction and cleaning, the dataset contains 9,542/1,052/992 QA instances for train/val/test, re-spectively (11,586 in total), following the official Audio-Grounding split protocol. Table 1 reports split statistics. Here, QA/Aud. measures the number of event-centered ques-tions per audio clip, Avg.#Seg reports temporal fragmenta-tion through the average number of annotated intervals, and Avg. Cov. (s) denotes the average total duration covered by the queried event on the approximately 10-second timeline. Appendix A gives construction prompts and QA examples. 

Models and Evaluation 

We fine-tune two LALMs, Qwen2.5-Omni-7B (Xu et al. 2025) and Qwen2-Audio-7B-Instruct (Chu et al. 2024), on the AudioGrounding-QA training split using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) (Hu et al. 2022). Qwen2.5-Omni is the primary model for detailed audio-token analysis, and Qwen2-Audio tests whether the pattern replicates across architec-tures. In both models, LoRA adapters are applied only inside the language model transformer blocks; the audio tower and multimodal audio to language projector remain frozen. Thus, acoustic encoding is fixed while the transformation of audio-token states inside the language model can change. Event Evidence Becomes More Accessible 

Figure 3 summarizes whether the event semantics identified in representation space are readable by the language decoder, using the calibrated readout gain from Section 3. Figures 3(a1–a3) show that fine-tuning increases both mean readout gain and coverage of positive tokens, with the largest differences in early and middle layers. Mean readout gain attenuates toward zero in later layers, whereas cover-age of positive tokens declines but remains substantial. The Top-k analysis further shows that the largest gains remain concentrated among highly readable states, while the layer-wise profile is stable across values of k. Fine-tuning there-fore broadens decoder accessibility while concentrating its strongest effects in audio tokens with high responses. Figure 3(b) provides a token-level view for the same rep-resentative example analyzed in Figure 2(b). Positive read-out is sparse and fragmented in the base checkpoint, but becomes substantially denser after fine-tuning, particularly across early and middle layers. The increase spans a broad range of audio-token positions rather than a few isolated states. Together with Figure 2(b), this case shows that event evidence organized over largely shared temporal locations becomes more broadly accessible to the language decoder after adaptation. The bands coarse-grain the per-layer curves in Appendix C into early, middle, late, and near-output regions. Although the audio tower and projector remain frozen, haℓ (i) is produced by language-model blocks up to layer ℓ and can therefore differ across checkpoints. We extract this layer-ℓ audio-token hidden state from either checkpoint under the original audio-question context, patch it into the same layer of either check-point’s diagnostic prompt, and let the remaining language-model layers complete the readout, yielding four patched-state and downstream-decoder combinations (Appendix C). Table 3 reveals clear downstream-decoder dominance. Swapping the downstream decoder yields positive gains from L1–L24, whereas swapping the checkpoint that supplies the patched state has little effect; the gap confidence intervals ex-clude zero in the first three bands. These results answer RQ2: the early- and middle-layer gains in Figure 3 arise primarily from fine-tuned decoder access rather than from differences between base and fine-tuned intermediate states. Both effects approach zero in L25–L28, where little downstream compu-tation remains after insertion. 

Output Consistency and Functional Relevance 

To connect diagnostic readout with timestamp genera-tion, we test the pathway inside the original generation context. On 100 fine-tuned Qwen2.5-Omni test instances, the temporal-window probes from Section 3 distinguish annotated-window recoverability from consistency with each checkpoint’s own predicted temporal support. Residual-delta erasure then compares predicted-window audio-token masks with size-matched random audio-token masks. Figures 4(a1,a2) reveal a complementary contrast between annotated-window discrimination and predicted-window consistency. For annotated windows, both checkpoints re-main above chance and exhibit similar layerwise profiles, indicating little change in linear recoverability after fine-tuning. In contrast, the base predicted-window probe remains near chance across layers, whereas the fine-tuned checkpoint achieves substantially higher AUROC over most of the net-work. The position-only control in Appendix E also remains close to chance, showing that this separation is not explained by token order alone. Thus, fine-tuning improves alignment with predicted support more than recoverability of annotated event windows, answering RQ3. 5 Discussion 

Latent Event Evidence (RQ1/RQ3). The results suggest that grounding fine-tuning does not simply create event evidence from scratch. Fine-tuning substantially improves timestamp prediction, yet the base checkpoint already con-tains measurable event-related structure. The audio tokens most strongly aligned with the queried event appear at simi-lar temporal positions before and after grounding fine-tuning, and annotated-window membership is weakly but consis-tently recoverable in the base checkpoint. Thus, poor ground-ing before fine-tuning is better explained by a weak mapping from existing event evidence to temporal outputs than by the absence of such evidence. 

Layered Semantics-to-Readout (RQ2/RQ3). Adapta-tion appears to induce a layered division of labor rather than a single-layer bottleneck. Early and middle layers show the strongest gains in decoder accessibility: calibrated readout becomes stronger and more widespread, and the cross-checkpoint control attributes these gains primarily to decoder-side adaptation. Later layers show the closest cor-respondence between query-relevant semantic evidence and predicted temporal support, suggesting tighter coupling to output-facing timing decisions in later computation. Query-side activation patching in Appendix F supports this progres-sion: phrase-level influence appears earlier, whereas answer-facing start-time recovery emerges closer to the output. The Qwen2-Audio replication shows the same broad transition from latent evidence to improved readability and predic-tion alignment, although the layerwise concentration remains model dependent. 

Output Consistency and Relevance (RQ3/RQ4). The re-sults separate three increasingly stringent notions of temporal evidence: recoverability, output consistency, and generation-time relevance. The annotated-window probe measures whether temporal membership is recoverable from audio-token states, whereas the predicted-window probe measures consistency between those states and each checkpoint’s own temporal outputs. Neither probe alone establishes that the recovered information affects generation. Residual-delta era-sure addresses this gap: removing updates within predicted windows degrades timestamp generation more than removing random updates of the same size. This intervention does not identify a complete circuit, but it narrows the functional test to predicted-window residual updates during generation. The agreement among recoverability, output consistency, and the intervention during generation supports the conclusion that fine-tuning improves how existing event evidence is read out and coupled to output timing. 

6 Conclusion 

Temporal grounding provides a diagnostic setting for exam-ining how language-model fine-tuning affects the semantics, decoder accessibility, and temporal output alignment of na-tive audio-token states. In both LALMs studied, base models contain latent event evidence, while fine-tuning strengthens decoder readout and consistency with predicted windows. Residual-delta erasure further shows that predicted-window updates contribute to timestamp generation. Together, these findings support a semantics-to-readout account in which supervision improves access to existing event evidence and connects it more reliably to temporal outputs. Thus, temporal grounding acts as both an evaluation target and a compact probe of decoder-accessible audio evidence.

# A Dataset Construction 

This section provides additional details for constructing AudioGrounding-QA, the temporal audio question-answering dataset used in the main paper. The dataset is derived from the public Audio-Grounding corpus, where each audio-caption sample contains phrase-level temporal annotations. Our conversion keeps the original temporal supervision intact while recasting each phrase item as an instruction-style question-answering instance. 

A.1 AudioGrounding-QA Conversion 

Each source AudioGrounding record contains an audio identifier, an AudioCaps identifier, a cap-tion, and phrase annotations with one or more temporal segments. We expand each valid phrase annotation into one QA instance. A valid item must have a non-empty source phrase and at least one numeric interval whose start time is no larger than its end time. The conversion preserves the original phrase-level temporal supervision while replacing caption-fragment queries with concise event-centered questions. 

A.2 Event-Phrase Normalization 

The original phrase annotations are caption spans and may be fragments rather than natural event expressions. We therefore normalize each source phrase into a concise event expression used in questions. The normalization is performed with Qwen3-Max using temperature 0 and JSON-object response format. Results are cached by lower-cased source phrase so repeated phrases receive the same normalized event text. 1Prompt component Content

The normalization is intentionally conservative: for example, a dog is barking becomes adog barking , while distressed cat growling is kept unchanged. 

A.3 Question Templates and Timestamp Answers 

For each valid phrase item, we generate one QA instance. The question is created from one of three templates: 1. When does {event } occur in this audio? 

2. At what time does {event } happen in this audio? 

3. During which time interval can {event } be heard in this audio? 

The template is selected deterministically from audio id and source phrase : the builder computes an MD5 hash of audio id || source phrase , maps it modulo 3, and adds 1 to obtain a template id. This keeps template variation reproducible without using randomness. Temporal segments are rounded to two decimals using decimal ROUND HALF UP rounding. The answer is then verbalized directly from the rounded intervals. For a single interval, the answer format is:     

> The event occurs from {start }to {end }seconds.

For multiple intervals, the intervals are joined with commas and a final and . For example: 

> The event occurs from 1.51 to 2.76 seconds, from 4.62 to 5.91 seconds, from 6.88 to 7.16 seconds, and from 8.28 to 9.15 seconds.

The diagnostic uses an event-phrase semantic bank as the textual reference space for target event alignment. In our implementation, the event-expression inventory contains 5,228 unique normalized expressions from the AudioGrounding-QA construction pool. # C Audio-Token Readout Diagnostics 

The main paper uses audio-token readout to separate event semantics in representation space from semantics readable by the decoder; this section specifies the diagnostic prompt used for that measurement. The readout diagnostic uses a separate prompt from the dataset construction prompt 5above. To obtain source audio-token states, we run the model on the original audio question input and append the answer prefix The event occurs from . This keeps hidden state extraction aligned with the temporal grounding generation format. To test event semantics readable by the decoder, we then use a text diagnostic prompt with a placeholder token. The selected audio-token state is patched into the placeholder position Sound . The string [PATCH] in the main-paper overview figure is a schematic label for this operation rather than the literal token used in implementation. The same diagnostic prompt is also decoded without patching to obtain the no-patch baseline used in the calibrated readout gain.

D.2 Evaluation Generation, Parsing, and Metrics 

For benchmark evaluation, the model receives the audio clip and event-centered question, then gen-erates a timestamp answer on the test split. We use float16 inference, batch size 2 where batching is supported, max new tokens=96 , and deterministic decoding with do sample=false ; temperature ,

top p , and top k are unset. For Qwen2.5-Omni, we disable audio output and evaluate only the textual timestamp response. Unless otherwise stated, each aggregate model or diagnostic result is computed from one fixed base or fine-tuned checkpoint and one deterministic evaluation pass on the stated split; reported confidence intervals use paired-instance resampling over fixed evaluation sets rather than repeated training runs. Predicted intervals are extracted from the generated text with a rule-based parser covering com-mon timestamp forms, including from ... to ... seconds , generic numeric ranges, between ... and ... , bracketed pairs, JSON-style start/end fields, and mm:ss ranges. Parsed intervals are normalized so that start time is no larger than end time, then overlapping intervals are merged. A prediction is counted as format-valid if at least one interval is parsed. Table 9 reports the format-valid rate on the 992-instance test split. Both base checkpoints already produce parseable timestamp responses for almost all test instances, and fine-tuning moves this rate only slightly to 100%. Thus, the behavioral gains in the main paper should not be read as only a change in parser success; the token-level diagnostics examine how event evidence is represented, read out, and aligned with generated temporal windows after a timestamp response is parseable.

E.3 Temporal-Window Probe Implementation 

For the Qwen2.5-Omni token-level probe analysis, we export hidden states from all 28 Thinker layers. Audio-token positions are identified with the model’s audio-token index, and each layer 11 produces one hidden vector for every audio token. We store the resulting audio-token vectors together with token-level metadata and train one independent linear probe per layer. To assign an approximate time span to each audio token, let an audio clip have duration T and 

n audio tokens. The token with rank r ∈ { 0, . . . , n − 1} is assigned center time T r/ (n − 1) and a clipped window of width approximately T /n . Ground-truth window labels mark whether this token window overlaps any annotated target event interval. Predicted window labels are constructed in the same way, but using intervals parsed from the model’s own generated response. Each probe uses standardized hidden vectors and a logistic linear classifier implemented as 

SGDClassifier with log loss, L2 regularization α = 10 −4, seed 42, and reweighting for positive tokens. The feature scaler is fitted on the training split by streaming over samples. For each layer, the decision threshold is selected on the validation split by searching thresholds from 0.05 to 0.95 in increments of 0.05, and the selected threshold is then evaluated on the held-out test split. Layerwise figures report held-out token-level AUROC across all layers. AUPRC is reported as an additional threshold-independent metric, while F1 and balanced accuracy are reported as complementary threshold-dependent diagnostics; the main paper uses held-out token-level AUROC as the primary layerwise probe metric. For the complementary single-layer summary in Table 11, we keep the validation-F1-selected layers and report all held-out metrics at those fixed layers.

# F Query-Side Activation Patching 

As an additional query-side intervention, we perform activation patching on clean/corrupted pairs that share the same audio but ask about different target events. In the corrupted run, we replace a selected hidden state with the corresponding hidden state from the clean run and then decode the timestamp answer. This keeps the acoustic evidence fixed while testing whether the patched query-conditioned state redirects the output toward the clean event. All activation-patching results use the fine-tuned Qwen2.5-Omni checkpoint. We form same-audio clean/corrupted pairs from the AudioGrounding-QA test split and orient each pair so the clean query is answered more accurately by the unpatched model. A pair is eligible when the clean baseline IoU is at least 0.30, the clean-minus-corrupted baseline IoU gap is at least 0.05, and the clean and corrupted ground-truth intervals have IoU at most 0.05. This screen yields 254 primary different-event candidates; we evaluate the top 100 pairs sorted by clean baseline IoU and clean-minus-corrupted gap. Patching is run for all 28 layers, layerwise curves average over the 100 pairs, and decoding is deterministic with max new tokens=128 and do sample=false . Both patching sites use the same generation suffix, The event occurs from , before decoding the timestamp answer. We compare two patching sites. Mean-phrase patching replaces the mean-pooled hidden repre-sentation of the target event phrase in the question, which serves as a phrase-level semantic anchor. Last-token patching replaces the hidden state at the final prompt token immediately before gener-ation, which serves as the answer-facing readout interface in the decoder-only thinker. Interpretation across models. Together, these Qwen2-Audio diagnostics provide a check across models on the main paper interpretation. As in Qwen2.5-Omni, fine-tuning strengthens semantics aligned with predictions, readout from selected tokens, and checkpoint-specific predicted-window AUROC, with little change in recoverability for ground-truth windows. The strongest shared pattern is therefore not that fine-tuning uniformly increases every form of event evidence, but that it makes event-relevant audio-token states more usable for the model’s own temporal grounding behavior. The main difference is distributional. Qwen2.5-Omni shows broader readout gains across audio-token states, whereas Qwen2-Audio concentrates readout gains in selected tokens with high re-sponse. We interpret this as variation across models in how event evidence readable by the decoder is distributed, not as a contradiction of the overall semantics-to-readout view. The evidence across models supports the qualitative claim while keeping the mechanism appropriately diagnostic rather than universal. These results should therefore be read as a matched diagnostic replication rather than a new training recipe or a claim that different LALM architectures must share the same layerwise pro-file. Reusing the same split, event-phrase inventory, matching cascade, readout prompt, SBERT scorer, and probe protocol keeps the comparison controlled, while building bank vectors specific to each model through its own text pathway keeps the semantic reference space appropriate for that architecture. Appendix G therefore complements the main Qwen2.5-Omni results by separating the shared diagnostic pattern from variation in the distribution of readable audio-token evidence across models. 18
