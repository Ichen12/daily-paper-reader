Abstract 

Omni models transcribe clean, single-speaker speech well, but their accuracy drops sharply when speakers overlap and the scene is noisy, exactly where knowing who said what matters most. A natural fix is a short scene description. We show why this is risky: answer-bearing text lets the model copy instead of listen, so the score rises although nothing has been heard; a silent test exposes this shortcut at once. We call this failure mode perception bypass and address it with 

Audio-Grounded Scaffold Context (AGSC) . AGSC links three steps: first, we build clues from audio to guide listening without giving the answer; second, answer-overlap and silence tests probe them for leakage and audio dependence; finally, those clues scaffold training but vanish at test time, yielding no-clue capability. Across three heterogeneous Omni models, training on AGSC lowers no-clue capped mean permutation word error rate (mpWER) on overlapping, noisy speech from 

25% –71% to 9% –15% . For streaming control, we formulate a joint GDPO task in which the model learns when to use a clue and how to produce a speaker-attributed transcript from sep-arately normalized format, gate, and transcript rewards. After internalization, AGSC adds almost no inference overhead. 

# Introduction 

Imagine a meeting recording in which two people briefly speak at once over the hum of an air conditioner. An Omni model is a multimodal large language model that handles text, images, audio, and video in one backbone (Xu et al. 2025; Cui et al. 2026; Inclusion AI et al. 2025b). It may transcribe the clean parts well and then fail at the overlap: it merges two voices, drops a speaker, or treats noise as words. These conditions are common in meetings, call centers, in-car assistants, and interviews, where knowing who said what is hardest and most useful. Textual scene descriptions seem like a natural aid when audio is unclear. We tried the most direct version and sup-plied a full transcript as context. The mean accuracy of three Omni models rose from about 53% to 94% . The jump was too good to be true. On the primary-transcript task, the two stronger models copied a wrong contextual answer 94% –

99 .8% of the time; the weakest copied it 47% of the time.

With silence in place of audio, all three still reached 100% 

primary-transcript accuracy. The high score measured copy-ing, not better listening. We call this failure mode perception bypass and turn it into a pass-or-fail test for context quality. This observation is our starting point. We hypothesize that Omni models already have useful acoustic capabilities that remain underused in complex scenes. Audio-Grounded Scaf-fold Context (AGSC) has two goals: draw out those capabil-ities and keep text from mimicking better listening. Its clues give only limited scene structure, and a silent-audio control checks whether the clue alone can still succeed without the audio signal. We test this idea with AGSC (Figure 1). Instead of telling the model what was said, AGSC supplies three kinds of scaf-folding: target-speaker activity, scene noise level, and partial, shuffled words. Each clue should guide listening, not become a text-only solution. To limit or expose context-only short-cuts, we use three safeguards: an audio-first instruction , an 

answer-overlap screen , and a silent-audio control . Stan-dard tools build the clues from raw audio. We use two training paths so the learned behavior is not tied to a fixed test-time prompt. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) mixes scaffolded and plain inputs; after training, we remove clues and treat the retained no-clue gain as a behavioral defi-nition of internalization . Group reward-decoupled normal-ization policy optimization (GDPO) (Liu et al. 2026) han-dles streaming control with separate rewards for output for-mat, gate decisions, and transcript quality. Gate-only training learns whether and when to use a clue; full-chain training learns the gate and speaker-attributed transcript jointly. Both paths update low-rank adapters. We make three contributions. (1) We identify perception bypass and turn silent audio into a behavioral design test for context. (2) We introduce AGSC (an automatic synthesis pipeline) and Context-Speech Bench (CSB) , which covers overlap, noise, meetings, streaming clues, and gating. (3) We show internalization: supervised training improves no-clue transcription, and one GDPO stage jointly trains gate and transcript outputs with streaming clues from 160 streams. # Related Work 

Audio-language models join an audio encoder with a large language model. They range from Qwen-Audio (Chu et al. 2023) and SALMONN (Tang et al. 2024) to Omni systems that understand and generate several modalities (Xu et al. 2025; Cui et al. 2026; Inclusion AI et al. 2025a,b). Speaker-attributed transcription remains difficult under overlap and noise (Kanda et al. 2020; Cornell et al. 2024). AIR-Bench, MMAU, and Dynamic-SUPERB mainly test broad listen-ing and reasoning ability (Yang et al. 2024; Sakshi et al. 2025; Huang et al. 2024). They do not directly test whether textual context leaks the answer. We add this control to Qwen3-Omni, MiniCPM-o, and Ming-flash-omni (hence-forth Qwen3, MiniCPM, and Ming). The classical route chains diarization (Bredin 2023), voice activity detection (Silero Team 2024), automatic speech recognition (ASR) (Radford et al. 2023), and sometimes sep-aration (Subakan et al. 2021); early errors can spread. A sec-ond route trains one model to emit several speakers (Kanda et al. 2020), uses speaker profiles (Medennikov et al. 2020), or targets distant meetings (Cornell et al. 2024). We train low-rank adaptation (LoRA) modules on the attention pro-jections and leave the pretrained weights unchanged (Hu et al. 2022). We reuse the front-end tools in a different role: they produce clues that the model must still check against audio, not answers it can copy. We keep a front-end component only when measurements show that it helps. Our focus is context design and parameter-efficient adaptation of Omni models, not a separate transcription cascade. Retrieval-augmented generation adds text from an external memory (Lewis et al. 2020), while chain-of-thought prompt-ing exposes intermediate reasoning steps (Wei et al. 2022). Contextual biasing gives a recognizer trusted word lists, such as contact names (Pundak et al. 2018). Our candidate words are different: they are automatic, possibly wrong, partial, and shuffled. Text-only systems can also rewrite recognition or diarization outputs (Chen et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024), but they cannot use the audio to correct a fluent but wrong draft. AGSC instead keeps audio as the source of the answer and limits context to partial clues that the model must check against the signal. Perception bypass is a form of shortcut learning (Geirhos et al. 2020). Our silent test has a role similar to language-prior controls used to rebalance visual question answering datasets (Goyal et al. 2017). Related failures also occur in vision-language and audio-video models (Deng et al. 2025; Nishimura, Nakada, and Kondo 2024; Leng et al. 2025). Recent work shows that large audio models can answer from text alone and uses that signal to split training data (He et al. 2026). We instead use silence as a design criterion and an empirical control: context should fail without audio. Context distillation moves the benefit of instructions or scratchpads into model weights (Snell, Klein, and Zhong 2022). We ask a related question: does clue-conditioned training improve no-clue evaluation, and can a model learn when a clue would be useful? Interference-Resilient Adap-tive Fusion (IRAF) studies a related problem in full-duplex dialogue, where an interfering speaker enters the user micro-phone (Zhong et al. 2026). It predicts input reliability and suppresses an unreliable signal. Both IRAF and our method use a learned gate, but at different points. IRAF reduces in-terference before fusion; we keep every speaker and gate a limited-content text clue. Its continuous gate provides a useful comparison with our discrete clue gate. 

# Method 

AGSC has three linked steps: it synthesizes answer-incomplete clues from audio, screens them for direct answer exposure and audio dependence, and uses them as remov-able training scaffolds. A separate GDPO study learns a joint gate-plus-transcript output for streaming control.

## AGSC: Clues, Not Answers 

This rule leads directly to a simple design principle: a clue may point and narrow, but it must not disclose the answer. AGSC applies this principle to each acoustic scene. For overlap, AGSC provides a time window for each speaker, such as target active 0.00–3.85 s, female , but no words. For single-speaker noise, it provides a coarse noise level, speech regions, and a partial, shuffled word list. We keep about two thirds of the candidate words and remove exact answers such as digits. For overlap plus noise, a sep-arator produces a rough draft for each voice. AGSC keeps half of the content words, adds up to four distractors from other recordings, removes duplicates, and shuffles the result. It never shows the full draft. Figure 1(b) shows this format. Missing words, distractors, and lost word order reduce di-rectly copyable content. The clue type matches the uncertainty. A time window ad-dresses who and when ; coarse attributes describe the listening condition; and a lossy word set narrows lexical alternatives. Removing sentence order, completeness, and exact-value to-kens keeps the clue directional rather than answer-like. An overlap-plus-noise clue therefore labels its candidates as par-tial, shuffled, and possibly wrong, omits much of the draft, and never presents a complete sentence. Three safeguards limit or expose shortcuts. The audio-first instruction marks every clue as automatic and fallible. The answer-overlap screen rejects a directly copyable an-swer string, while the silent-audio control tests whether the remaining clue can still replace sound. Clean audio does not need a clue, so a gate adds one only for detected overlap or low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). The gate handles a separate risk: a non-answer clue can still distract a segment that needs no help. 

## Automatic Synthesis Pipeline 

The automatic pipeline turns raw audio into AGSC. It di-arizes speakers (Bredin 2023), runs segment ASR to obtain candidate words, and detects overlap, speech regions (Silero Team 2024), and a coarse SNR level. For overlap plus noise, SepFormer separates the voices (Subakan et al. 2021); per-speaker ASR then supplies words for shuffling. The gate decides whether to add a clue, and the exporter records the clue with a confidence tag. Each component must earn its place through measurement. We select the diarizer with lower diarization error rate (DER, 

0.22 versus 0.47 ) and remove an acoustic-scene tagger with only 29% accuracy. SNR is useful only as a coarse level: that level is correct 79% of the time, although the point estimate has 9.9 dB mean error. On twelve evaluation recordings, the full pipeline reaches DER 0.15 , finds the correct speaker count for 83% of clips, and obtains draft character error rate (CER) 0.20 . The confidence tag indicates how much the model should trust the front end rather than presenting an uncertain clue as ground truth.

# Experiments 

We test three Omni models: Qwen3-Omni 30B (Xu et al. 2025), MiniCPM-o 4.5 (9B) (Cui et al. 2026), and Ming-flash-omni 2.0, a 100B-total, 6.1B-active-per-token mixture-of-experts model (Inclusion AI et al. 2025b). CSB covers overlap, noise, meetings, streaming clue matu-rity, and gating. Its English speech comes from LibriSpeech and SparseLibriMix2 (Panayotov et al. 2015; Cosentino et al. 2020); Mandarin comes from AISHELL-3 mixes (Shi et al. 2021); noise comes from WHAM and the Music, Speech, and Noise (MUSAN) corpus (Wichern et al. 2019; Snyder, Chen, and Povey 2015); and meetings come from the Aug-mented Multi-party Interaction (AMI) Meeting Corpus (Car-letta et al. 2006). Gold answers and predicted clues are stored separately. CSB contains 13 ,255 training and 1,525 evalua-tion samples. It also has dedicated splits for gate-only rein-forcement ( 120 streams; 240 paired windows), full-chain re-inforcement ( 160 streams), clue maturity ( 385 prefix clues), and real-time gating ( 30 streams). The supplement gives the split table and construction details. We report mpWER, Mandarin CER, and gating F 1 (the harmonic mean of precision and recall). # Discussion 

Our hypothesis is simple: some complex-scene failures re-flect an underused capability, not an absent one. AGSC tries to draw out that capability without turning its clues into textual shortcuts. During SFT, an incomplete target window, condition tag, or candidate list acts as a temporary trigger. We call the lower error that remains after removing the trigger re-tained activation . Answer-overlap screening limits copying; the silent-audio control measures performance after silenc-ing the audio. Each check answers a different question: context-on eval-uation asks whether a clue helps; silence asks whether it can replace audio; no-clue evaluation asks whether behavior persists after scaffold removal; and streaming metrics ask whether assistance is timed correctly. No check substitutes for another. We use internalization for post-training persis-tence without clues. SFT targets persistence after scaffold removal; GDPO sep-arates gate-only timing from full-chain gate-plus-transcript training under separately normalized rewards. How useful a clue is depends on the scene and on how much prefix audio was available to build it. A wordless win-dow can narrow target location, while Figure 4 shows non-monotonic, model-specific changes as prefix evidence grows. Table 2 also shows that an unconditional clue after training need not beat the no-clue path. There is no universal prompt. Our predefined templates match clue type to the diagnosed ambiguity, and the gate limits when a clue enters. Together, the results separate what persists without run-time help (no-clue mpWER), when a clue should enter (gate metrics), and transcript accuracy under joint training (full-chain mpWER). Runtime clues remain in the streaming path, keeping assistance optional and failures visible. 

# Conclusion 

This paper turns a tempting shortcut into a design principle. Answer-bearing context can let a model score without listen-ing; we call this perception bypass and expose it by silenc-ing audio. That test anchors three contributions: AGSC uses answer-incomplete scaffolds to guide listening while limit-ing direct copying; CSB evaluates them with explicit shortcut controls; and internalization is judged by whether gains per-sist after clues are removed. After SFT, no-clue capped mean mpWER on overlap plus noise falls from 25% –71% to 9% –

15% , providing operational evidence of internalization. For streaming, gate-only GDPO learns whether and when to use a clue; full-chain GDPO jointly trains the gate and speaker-attributed transcript. Future work will extend scene-matched clues to new scene types and test the learned gate under do-main shift. The deeper principle is simple: context should scaffold perception, not replace it.
